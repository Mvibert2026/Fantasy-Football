Consensus order, your league size and your draft slot. That is the whole input list.
It does not use what your managers did in previous drafts. Every figure on this screen is a range. The width is availability.noise_band, measured by refitting the model 200 times over resampled draft logs — not a padding we chose. Read the band, not the midpoint: 20–44% is meaningfully different from 60–84%, and not meaningfully different from 26–50%.

STRATEGY GUIDE GENERATED 22 JUL 2026

Drafting from slot 3 in a 10-team, half-PPR, no-kicker league

{{ r.label }}
{{ r.ours }} {{ r.market }}
league.replacement_levels vs. the public default — struck-through value is what a public board assumes

Your board differs from a public board for exactly one reason, and it is not an opinion. Three WR slots, two flexes and no kicker move replacement level to RB28 / WR41 / TE11 / QB10, and every rank on this board follows from that arithmetic. We do not carry a second, evaluative component: players at the same positional rank get the same projection from us, because we have never demonstrated an edge that would justify separating them.

The plan at 3, 18 and 23

3 PICK
ROUND 1 · BEST AVAILABLE
Take the best player available. At slot 3 you are choosing between two or three players who are all inside tier 1, and no format correction is large enough to override that. Do not think about positional scarcity here; think about it at 18.
18 PICK
ROUND 2 · SCARCITY TURN
This is a 15-pick gap followed by a 5-pick gap. Anything you pass on at 18 has to survive four picks to reach you at 23 — and two of those belong to the managers who took a TE in round 3 last season. Take the position that will not survive; you can take value at 23.
23 PICK
ROUND 3 · TIER DECISION
The elite-TE decision usually resolves here. If a tier-1 TE is on the board, the measured edge over the next tier is 41 points a season — the largest single positional edge in your format. If both are gone, take the best WR and do not chase.

League-specific corrections

With 10 teams, 3 WR slots, 2 flexes and no kicker, the last startable player at each position sits at RB28 / WR41 / TE11 / QB10. Published boards assume a 12-team league with RB24 / WR36 and a kicker occupying a roster spot. The practical consequences: WR depth is worth more than it looks, RB scarcity is milder than it looks, the TE cliff is steeper because only eleven of them ever start, and the roster spot everyone else spends on a kicker is yours to spend on a sixth bench player.

WHAT WE TESTED AND FOUND NOTHING

Public guides never report their failures. These are ours, and they are the reason to trust the numbers above: the same machinery that produced the positive results produced these, and we are not hiding them.

Spike weeks are not a player trait
We tested whether certain players reliably produce ceiling weeks. Across 26 seasons of game logs there is no detectable player-level persistence — last year's spike-week reputation predicts nothing about this year's. Nothing to draft on.
Hero RB has no measurable advantage
Hero RB against best-available came out at −0.4% of roster points, well inside the noise band, positive in 2 of 4 seasons. It is neither a strategy nor a mistake. Draft the board.
Reaching early for TE or QB costs 3–5%
This one is a finding rather than a null: reaching a full round early for either position cost 3–5% of roster points, consistently, in every season tested. Note the distinction from the positive TE result above — taking an elite TE at his price is good; paying a round of premium for the certainty is not.
